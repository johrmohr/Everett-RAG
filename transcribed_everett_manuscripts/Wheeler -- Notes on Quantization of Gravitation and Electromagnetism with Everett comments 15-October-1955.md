# Wheeler -- Notes on Quantization of Gravitation and Electromagnetism with Everett comments 15-October-1955.pdf

NOTES ON QUANTIZATION OF GRAVITATION AND ELECTROMAGNETISM (Outgrowth of conversationswith Charles Misner and Joseph Weber; motivation partly to summarize present state of our thinking, partly to lead up to Joseph Weber's proposal to investigate quantization of electromagnetism in a multiply connected continuum. - - J.A.W. - - 15 October 1955)  

Selection of a particular framework of ideas from among several possibilities Possibility (1) - - To forget temporarily the electromagnetic field and look at how to do the sum over histories for the metric field. Under investigation by Charles Misner. Expect that appropriate volume element for history integration will be defined by the action principle of general relativity itself, as in the more elementary examples discussed by C. Herette, Phys. Rev. 21, 849 (1951). Consequently anticipate definability of volume element by means of techniques of differential geometry. The issue is not so much the do- ability of the definition, as the do- ability of the integration. The non- linearity of gravitation theory makes one fear the worst. Contrast this non- linear theory with electromagnetism. There the integration goes like a dream. The exponent is quadratic in the field variables. The answer is the classical action for the classical history that leads from the initial to the final field configuration. Hopes for such a simple outcome in the non- linear gravitation problem may be low, but should absolutely not be abandoned. The action principle there is founded on the most simple geometrical notions. Consequently the answer to the integration ought also to be simple. After all, what can it depend on? Only on simple invariants. By exploiting this line of reasoning one might be able to get the answer by no calculation at all. As a preliminary way to test this possible approach, one can try it on electromagnetism itself, where one knows the answer. Possibility (2) To make the machinery of the discussion still more abstract, but to keep the general plan and scope the same as in possibility (1). By "abstract" is here meant a mathematical machinery that bears the same relation to the many alternative coordinate systems of general relativity that the abstract theory of groups bears to the theory of group representations. Possibility (3): A still deeper formulation where one deals with a Borel set,  or bucket of dust, as starting point. One hopes for the following: A very  abstract action principle defined over this set. A quantum mechanics done by  a sum over all field values at this set of points. This sum - - one hopes - -  is in a certain approximate sense equivalent to a sum over manifolds of all  dimensionalities. 3 + 1 dimensions contribute "more" to this sum than any other  dimensionality by virtue of the special properties of this dimensionality.  The contribution is in a certain approximate sense equivalent to the action  principle of gravitation plus electromagnetism. The structure of the neighbor-  hood of each point in the approximately (3+1) dimensional manifold - - the fact  that this neighborhood is built out of points in the Borel set - - requires for  its description a Lorentz transformation at each point independent of that at  every other point (spinor field). This way of looking at things may give a  foundation to Possibilities (1) and (2), but is for the present more a hope than  a concrete idea. 

Possibility (4), midway between (1) and (2), would use the canonical coordinates  of Komar to express the content of relativity and the sum over histories.  Probably not too convenient for this particular purpose, though very well adapted  to study inhomogeneities in the dynamics of the metric (analogues in general  relativity of the Taylor and Helmholtz instability that one has in hydrodynamics).  Possibility (5) for a framework of ideas for further investigation (discussed  October 12 with Joseph Weber): Consider the metric field to be prescribed  (either constant or changing in a given way with time), and study the quantisation  of the electromagnetic field.

Motivations - - To get singularity- free description of electric charges.  

To get better understanding of the electromagnetic field. To see if there is any way to visualize reason for charge quantization in a rather limited framework of ideas. To explore consequences of multiple connectivity. (Joseph Weber stresses analogue to Kirchoff point of view as it is applied to electric circuits.) Convenient circumstances - - The circumstance that the Lagrangian is quadratic in the field variables; the ease in doing the summation over field histories at once; the advantage that the electromagnetic field, being described by an antisymmetric tensor of the second rank, fits naturally into the formalism of fibre bundles and sheaves, not to mention the formalism of exterior differential forms. Additional notes following phone conversation with Joseph Weber on October 15 

(1) Regarding the 3-dimensional block of cheese in the last example, it is easy to accept the idea of a coordinate patch that takes one from \(z=0\) back to \(z=0\) and of similar patches that connect \(y=0\) with \(y=0\), or \(x=0\) with \(x=0\). Not so obvious, but equally simple in principle, is a coordinate patch that connects the regions adjacent to the two holes in the Swiss cheese. Let spherical coordinates of a typical point in the cheese around the first hole → referred to the center of that hole → be called 

\[r_1 = a(1 + u_1); \quad e_1; \quad y_1\]

and let similar quantities measure the coordinates of a point immediately around the second worm hole, referred to the center of that worm hole. Then introduce a coordinate patch system, \(u, \theta, \varphi\) as follows: Points with \(u\) between 0 and 0.1 lie just around the first hole, and have coordinates 

\[r_1 = a(1 + u) = a(1 + u); \quad e_1 = e; \quad y_1 = y. \quad \text{Points with } u \text{ from } -0.1 \text{ to } 0 \text{ lie just around the second hole, and have coordinates}\]

\[r_2 = a(1 + u_2) = a(1 - u); \quad e_2 = \pi - e; \quad y_2 = y. \quad \text{An ant eating his way}\]

(2) Regarding the time in the last example, Joseph Weber points out it would be as easy to make time periodic as to make space periodic. Then the outcome of the example will however be quite different. If the periods are transcendentally related (J. A. W. discussions with C. M. and P. C.) there will in general be no solutions in the case where there are no holes. It will be a special case to have any solutions at all for the wave equation even when the holes are present. (Also see Gödel, Rev. Mod. Phys. 1949 for spaces periodic in time.) (3) In a manifold periodic in time, it should be possible for a "handle" or "worm hole" to be towed around many times, each time making a slight displacement in space. Thus an observer at one time should see a great number of worm holes. Their shape and separation may have changed at each circuit of the time dimension. But the electromagnetic flux through the worm hole cannot change (C. M.). Accordingly, one arrives at the interesting picture of a number of charges identical in magnitude, though different in shape (metric) and in other physical characteristics. Is such periodicity in time the only way one can get the identity of electric charges in a natural way? 

(4) Regarding the polarizability of a geon as already calculated by Joseph Weber, and its possible relationship to the polarizability of elementary particles, he points out that the polarizability of particles offers one perhaps a first hand-hold on how electromagnetic forces behave inside elementary particles. This question is relevant when one is investigating whether nuclear forces are a manifestation of electromagnetic forces under conditions when radiative and fluctuational components of the field dominate over the usual static components. The idea is analogous to that expressed by Debye, when he proposed that chemical forces come from electrostatic forces, unacceptable as that idea seemed at the

---

time. How valence and degree of ionization from a single entity? Similarly, today, how explain charge and isotopic spin and heavy particle number and strangeness index of elementary particles? 

(5) To understand polarizability of elementary particles a little better, Joseph Weber is calculating the minimum value (in cm³) detectable with an electric field of \(10^6\) volts/cm and a field gradient of \(10^7\) volts/cm², acting for 1 meter on a particle moving with thermal velocity, when a deflection of 0.1 mm is observable. 

(6) The polarizability of an atom is easily defined -- the relevant distances, \(h^2/m_e^2\), are great in comparison with the uncertainty nucleus; \(h/m_e\) for a proton. Even for a neutron the polarizability is presumably associated with a distance \(h/m_e\), say 10 times greater than the uncertainty limit in the localizability of the neutron. But for a free electron both distances are probably of the order \(h/m_e\). Consequently it is not clear that one can define for an electron the separation between the electric and mass centers of gravity, even in principle. This point deserves careful investigation.

Notes and corrigenda (with thanks to Charles Misner):  

(1) It is of course not the implication that one needs the usual notation of tensor calculus in order to define Betti numbers. Rather, Professor Spencer argues that neither the tensor calculus, nor the Cartan calculus, nor Betti numbers reflect adequately the properties of the space. They fail to suggest ideas richer than those of Betti numbers, ideas which may be buried in the theory. He thinks that formulating the theory in terms of sheaves may focus our attention on more profitable concepts.  

(2) D. C. Spencer hoped that passing from an homology theory based on integer coefficients (where one just counts the non-trivial surfaces that one can imagine or draw) to an homology theory using a sheaf of coefficients which would be tied to the analytic structure of the field theory one might find a subtler structure than imaginable surfaces (although perhaps related) which would show the desired continuous variation.  

(3) Charles Misner emphasizes that metric is needed to define divergence even when divergence is zero. He also points out that the statement ascribed to him should be corrected. He notes that analytic function theory and electrostatics are identical when a scalar potential can be introduced. But, if the analogue of charge is to be introduced by finding circles through which a flux passes there can not exist a non-singular potential. There may still be function theoretic tools available, but what they are is not clear. Seg, howover, Pierre Conner.
