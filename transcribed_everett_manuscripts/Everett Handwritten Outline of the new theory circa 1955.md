It is also interesting, as a rule view, to give an example of the usefulness of the more general definition of relativistic information. Suppose we have a stochastic process with a finite number of states \(S\). Suppose that the process takes place at discrete times, and that the transition probability for going to the state \(i\) is \(T_{ij}\). The probabilities \(T_{ij}\) then form what is a stochastic matrix, i.e. the elements are between 0 and 1, and \(\sum T_{ij} = 1\). If at any time the system has a probability distribution \(P_i\) on the states, then at the next interval of time its distribution will be \(P'_i = \sum P_i T_{ij}\). On the special case where the matrix is doubly-stochastic, which means that both \(\sum T_{ij} = 1\) and \(\sum T_{ij} = 1\), and which amounts to a principle of statistical balancing holding, it is known that the entropy, defined as \(-\sum P_i \ln P_i\), is a monotonically decreasing function of states. This entropy, however, is simply the negative of the information, relative to the different pairs measure. One can extend this result to the general case of arbitrary stochastic matrices, if one defines the entropy to be the negative of the information relative to the stationary distribution measure. Thus the stationary distribution \(P^*\) is that which does not change with time, i.e. for which \(\sum P^* T_{ij} = P^*_{ij}\) so that the generating distribution becomes \(H = -\sum P_i \ln \frac{P_i}{P^*}\) which, it is easily shown, is a monotone increasing function of this time. Note that the stationary distribution of doubly stochastic matrices is the uniform distribution, which explains the theorem on the special case of doubly stochastic matrices.
