The amplification process operates upon the ability of some microsystem (particle, for example) to become correlated with the state of an enormous number of other microscopic systems, the totality of which we shall call a detection system. For example, the totality of gas atoms in a Geiger counter, on the water molecules in a cloud chamber, constitute such a detection system. 

The amplification is accomplished by arranging the condition of the detection system so that the states of the individual micro-systems of the detector are metastable, in a way that if one micro-system should fall from its metastable state it would influence the reduction of others. This type of arrangement leaves the entire detection system metastable against chain reactions until involve a large number of its constituent systems. On a

The chain reactions cause large numbers of the micro-systems of the detector to behave as a unit, all remaining in the metastable state, or all disintegrating. In this manner the state of a sufficiently large number of micro-systems are correlated, so that one can speak of the stable ensemble being in a state of discharge, or not.
